## PART 1: PROBLEM STATEMENT

### 1.2 Problem Statement

Wound management is a critical component of the healing process, particularly for patients with chronic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, venous insufficiency, and pressure injuries, who require frequent clinical assessments to monitor wound progression, maintain wound hygiene, and receive timely, appropriate treatment before complications arise. Globally, chronic wounds affect an estimated 6.5 million individuals in the United States alone, with annual treatment costs exceeding USD 25 billion and projected to rise with an ageing population and increasing prevalence of diabetes (Sen et al., 2009; Nussbaum et al., 2018). The mobility limitations common among these patient populations make routine in-person clinic visits burdensome, costly, and at times clinically impractical, particularly in resource-constrained or geographically remote settings where access to specialist wound care practitioners is limited (Amani et al., 2023).

The T.I.M.E. framework — assessing Tissue type, Infection or Inflammation status, Moisture balance, and wound Edge characteristics — was developed in 2002 and has since been widely adopted as a standardised clinical instrument for wound bed preparation and holistic wound assessment (Leaper et al., 2012). Despite its structured nature, the application of the T.I.M.E. framework in clinical practice remains predominantly manual. Healthcare professionals conduct wound assessments through direct visual observation and record findings by hand, a process that is time-consuming when scaled across multiple patients, highly dependent on individual clinical experience, and susceptible to inter-clinician variability in descriptive language, severity judgement, and dressing selection (Schultz et al., 2003). Such inconsistencies can directly compromise treatment continuity and wound healing outcomes, as suboptimal dressing selection — including incorrect moisture management, inappropriate use of antimicrobials, or failure to escalate infection signs — may lead to wound deterioration, systemic infection, or unnecessary limb loss (Kesaraju, 2025).

The increasing availability of wound photography in clinical and research settings has catalysed efforts to automate components of wound assessment through image processing and deep learning (Chen et al., 2024). Advances in wound tissue segmentation, infection classification, and multi-task image analysis have demonstrated the feasibility of automating discrete T.I.M.E.-aligned assessment tasks. However, the majority of existing systems address these tasks in isolation — producing tissue composition percentages or binary infection labels independently — rather than generating comprehensive T.I.M.E.-based outputs that can directly inform clinical dressing decisions (Moortgat et al., 2024). Furthermore, even where structured classification outputs are available, a critical downstream gap persists: the structured assessment findings must be translated into actionable, evidence-grounded wound dressing recommendations, a step that remains manual, inconsistent, and inaccessible for remote or underserved patients.

Current large language model (LLM) deployments in clinical settings face a persistent and well-documented challenge: generative models produce authoritative-sounding but factually unsupported or clinically unsafe outputs, a phenomenon commonly referred to as "hallucination" (Ji et al., 2023). In high-stakes domains such as wound care, where incorrect dressing selection, failure to escalate infection signs, or inappropriate antibiotic guidance can lead to serious patient harm, the deployment of ungrounded generative models presents unacceptable clinical risk (Potluri et al., 2025). Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) addresses this limitation by grounding model outputs in a curated, authoritative knowledge base of clinical guidelines rather than relying solely on the model's parametric weights (Lewis et al., 2020). However, the specific design decisions governing retrieval quality, prompt structure, clinical safety enforcement, and mobile deployment feasibility for wound care RAG systems remain underexplored in the existing literature (Bunnell et al., 2025).

This study therefore addresses that downstream gap within a collaborative end-to-end wound assessment pipeline. A companion system — developed by co-investigators — performs automated tissue type quantification (percentage composition of slough, granulation, and necrotic tissue) and IME classification (Infection/Inflammation, Moisture, and Edge) from wound photographs, producing structured categorical outputs that serve as the primary inputs to the system described herein. This division of labour allows the present study to focus entirely on the clinical decision support layer: developing, iteratively evaluating, and deploying a RAG-based wound dressing recommendation system — named VerdaSense — that accepts structured T.I.M.E. inputs together with optional free-text patient clinical notes and generates evidence-grounded wound dressing recommendations derived from established clinical wound care guidelines.

This study develops and systematically evaluates the VerdaSense RAG system through an iterative nine-version ablation study across two language model generators (GPT-4o-mini and Qwen3:14b), assessing each architectural configuration against four automated retrieval and generation quality metrics — Context Precision, Context Recall, Faithfulness, and Answer Relevancy (Es et al., 2023) — as well as a rule-based clinical safety checker enforcing dressing appropriateness, antibiotic recommendation accuracy, and referral criteria. The system is subsequently integrated into a smartphone-based wound care application as the clinical recommendation layer of the collaborative assessment pipeline, where its response latency and output usability under near real-time mobile deployment conditions are assessed.

The findings of this study contribute to the growing body of evidence on clinical RAG system design, with particular relevance to mobile health applications for wound care in resource-constrained or remote patient settings.

## PART 2: LITERATURE REVIEW

### 2.1 Background and Problem Context

Chronic wounds represent a significant and growing public health burden globally. Defined as wounds that fail to progress through the normal stages of healing within an expected timeframe — typically four to twelve weeks — chronic wounds encompass venous leg ulcers, diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), pressure injuries, and other complex wound types that disproportionately affect the elderly, patients with diabetes, and those with compromised vascular or immune function (Sen et al., 2009). The global prevalence of diabetic foot ulcers alone is estimated at 6.3%, with a lifetime risk approaching 25% for individuals with diabetes mellitus, and DFUs are associated with more hospital admissions than any other diabetes-related complication (Armstrong et al., 2017). In the United States, the direct annual cost of chronic wound care exceeds USD 28 billion, with projections indicating continued growth as the global diabetic population is forecast to reach 700 million by 2045 (Nussbaum et al., 2018; IDF, 2021). Despite this burden, access to specialist wound care assessment remains inequitable: patients in rural or resource-limited settings, and those with mobility restrictions common to chronic wound aetiologies, face significant barriers to regular in-person clinical review, leading to delayed identification of wound deterioration and suboptimal treatment outcomes (Amani et al., 2023; Saadati Fard et al., 2025).

The clinical management of chronic wounds requires systematic, structured assessment to guide treatment decisions. The T.I.M.E. framework, first developed in 2002 and presented at the European Wound Management Association meeting in 2003, provides a standardised instrument for wound bed preparation aligned with four core assessment axes: Tissue viability (classification of necrotic, sloughy, granulating, or epithelialising tissue), Infection or Inflammation status, Moisture balance (assessment of exudate level and type), and wound Edge characteristics (advancing versus non-advancing epithelium) (Schultz et al., 2003; Leaper et al., 2012). The T.I.M.E. framework has since been cited extensively in wound care guidelines, clinical protocols, and consensus documents, and was subsequently updated to the TIMERS model incorporating Repair/Regeneration and Social factors (Wounds International, 2022). Despite its widespread adoption, practical application of T.I.M.E. in clinical settings remains predominantly manual and subject to significant inter-clinician variability: individual differences in clinical experience, descriptive language, and threshold judgements for infection or moisture classification lead to inconsistent wound assessments and downstream inconsistencies in dressing selection (Kesaraju, 2025). These inconsistencies are particularly pronounced in community or primary care settings where wound care specialist input may not be readily available, and where healthcare personnel rotate frequently across patient cohorts (StatPearls, 2023).

### 2.2 Wound Care AI: Computer Vision, Classification, and Assessment Automation

The availability of digital wound photography has motivated substantial research into automating wound assessment components through image processing and machine learning. Early approaches focused on wound dimension measurement and area estimation from calibrated photographs, but the field has progressed significantly toward automated tissue type classification, infection detection, and multi-task image-level assessment (Chen et al., 2024). Deep learning approaches — including convolutional neural networks (CNNs), Vision Transformers (ViTs), and multimodal vision-language models (VLMs) — have demonstrated the feasibility of classifying wound tissue composition, detecting signs of infection, and estimating moisture level from wound images with accuracy levels approaching clinical inter-rater agreement in controlled experimental settings (Xu et al., 2025).

A notable development in the multimodal wound assessment literature is SCARWID (Xu et al., 2025), which combines image features with machine-generated textual captions derived from a wound VLM to produce explainable, multi-modal wound infection classification. SCARWID demonstrates that augmenting visual classification with automatically generated linguistic descriptions of wound appearance improves both classification accuracy and interpretability — a finding relevant to pipeline architectures such as VerdaSense where structured text descriptions (the T.I.M.E. labels from upstream classifiers) serve as the primary inputs to a downstream language-based recommendation system. The MEDIQA-WV 2025 Shared Task (Yim et al., 2025) further advanced the field by benchmarking seventeen competing systems on wound care visual question answering, combining wound images with patient text queries to produce free-text wound care responses. The top-performing approach (MasonNLP) used CLIP-based visual embeddings to retrieve visually similar wound exemplars and fed both exemplar text and the wound image into a vision-language model for generation, while the EXL Health AI Lab approach (Durgapraveen et al., 2025) used metadata-guided generation — classifying wound attributes from the image and injecting structured predictions into the generation prompt — which is architecturally analogous to the VerdaSense input mechanism. For home-based wound monitoring, Saadati Fard et al. (2025) proposed a multimodal AI system for patient referral decision support from wound photographs annotated by specialist nurses, finding that combining visual features with structured clinical metadata significantly improved referral recommendation accuracy over image-only approaches. These works collectively establish that the downstream gap — translating structured wound assessment outputs into actionable treatment recommendations — is both clinically important and underserved by existing systems, which primarily focus on classification outputs rather than guideline-grounded decision support.

### 2.3 Conceptual Definitions: Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Hallucination, and Clinical Grounding

**Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)** refers to a family of architectures in which a large language model's generation process is augmented by dynamically retrieving relevant documents or passages from an external knowledge base at query time (Lewis et al., 2020). Unlike fine-tuned models, which encode domain knowledge into model parameters during training and require expensive retraining when guidelines are updated, RAG systems maintain a modifiable external knowledge store — typically a vector database of semantically chunked documents — and retrieve the most relevant chunks via embedding similarity (dense retrieval), keyword matching (sparse retrieval, e.g., BM25), or hybrid combinations of both. The retrieved context is then injected into the LLM's generation prompt, enabling the model to produce responses grounded in specific, citable source passages rather than relying solely on its parametric memory. **Hallucination** in the clinical LLM context refers to the generation of statements that are factually incorrect, unsupported by retrieved evidence, or clinically unsafe, but presented with apparent authority and fluency (Ji et al., 2023). RAG addresses hallucination risk by enforcing that generation is constrained to retrieved guideline content, though it does not eliminate the risk: models may still misinterpret retrieved context, fail to retrieve relevant passages, or extrapolate beyond retrieved evidence under certain query conditions (Potluri et al., 2025). **Clinical grounding** describes the property of a generated response being attributable to specific, identifiable passages in authoritative clinical guidelines, such that the reasoning chain from retrieved evidence to recommendation is transparent and verifiable — a property distinct from mere factual accuracy and directly linked to clinical safety (Zakka et al., 2024). **Context Precision** measures the proportion of retrieved chunks that are relevant to the query (ranking quality of retrieval); **Context Recall** measures whether all ground-truth relevant information appears in the retrieved context; **Faithfulness** measures whether generated claims are attributable to retrieved passages; and **Answer Relevancy** measures whether the generated response addresses the clinical query (Es et al., 2023).

### 2.4 RAG in Clinical Medicine: Foundational Work and Convergent Evidence

The foundational study establishing RAG as a viable framework for clinical medicine is Almanac (Zakka et al., 2024), developed at Stanford Medicine. Almanac augmented a large language model with retrieval capabilities over a curated corpus of medical guidelines and treatment resources and evaluated performance on 314 clinical scenarios across nine medical specialties, assessed by a panel of eight board-certified clinicians. Almanac demonstrated significant improvements over standard LLMs — including ChatGPT-4, Bing, and Bard — across axes of factuality, completeness, user preference, and adversarial safety, establishing early evidence that guideline-grounded retrieval substantially improves clinical LLM reliability. The system's architecture — combining document retrieval with constrained generation — provided the conceptual template for subsequent clinical RAG work and remains a canonical reference. However, Almanac's evaluation focused on binary human preference ratings rather than fine-grained metric decomposition, precluding analysis of whether retrieval quality (recall, precision) or generation fidelity (faithfulness) was the dominant determinant of clinical accuracy improvements.

Subsequent work converged on automated evaluation as a practical necessity for iterative RAG development at scale. RAGAS (Es et al., 2023), introduced at EACL 2024, proposed a suite of four reference-free metrics — Faithfulness, Answer Relevancy, Context Precision, and Context Recall — that use an LLM-as-judge to evaluate RAG pipeline components without requiring manually labelled ground truth for every test instance. RAGAS evaluates these dimensions via LLM-based automated scoring, finding that its predictions for faithfulness and answer relevance are closely aligned with human judgements on the WikiEval benchmark. The widespread adoption of RAGAS across clinical RAG work — including the EULAR rheumatology RAG (Madrid-García et al., 2025), the NICE guideline RAG (Lewis et al., 2025), and RAGMed (Patil et al., 2025) — has established it as the de facto standard for automated RAG evaluation in the medical domain, enabling rapid architectural iteration without continuous manual clinician annotation. A critical limitation of RAGAS identified in clinical deployments is its sensitivity to how the user query is formulated: when structured data (such as categorical T.I.M.E. labels) is used as the RAGAS query input rather than a natural language question, Answer Relevancy scores are systematically underestimated, as the judge compares a rich clinical narrative response against a terse structured input. This measurement artefact has implications for ablation study comparisons where the query formulation changes across versions.

RAGMed (Patil et al., 2025) provides a broader synthesis perspective. Surveying RAG implementations across clinical domains including radiology, cardiology, oncology, and primary care, RAGMed finds consistent evidence that RAG-augmented LLMs outperform unaugmented baselines on factual accuracy and reduce hallucination rates in domain-specific clinical queries. The review further identifies three universal challenges across clinical RAG deployments: knowledge base coverage (small or outdated guideline corpora limiting system scope), retrieval precision (general-purpose embedding models underperforming on specialised clinical terminology), and safety enforcement (the absence of post-generation safety checks allowing clinically incorrect recommendations to reach patients even when retrieval succeeds). These challenges directly motivate the multi-component architecture of VerdaSense, which addresses knowledge base curation through targeted guideline ingestion, retrieval precision through medical-domain embedding models and hybrid retrieval, and safety enforcement through a dedicated rule-based clinical safety checker applied post-generation.

### 2.5 Domain-Specific Clinical RAG: Guideline Grounding, Architecture Design, and Safety

The most directly comparable published work to VerdaSense is the EULAR/ACR rheumatology RAG system (Madrid-García et al., 2025), which integrated seventy-four EULAR and ACR clinical guidelines into a RAG system using hybrid retrieval combining dense embeddings (voyage-3) with BM25 sparse retrieval and a Qdrant vector store, with ChatGPT o3-mini as generator and Gemini 2.0 Flash as LLM judge. Evaluated on 740 specialist-level questions, RAG outperformed unaugmented baseline LLMs on factual accuracy, safety, and completeness (P<0.001) in both automated and human evaluation, with RAG responses preferred in 92.8% of pairwise comparisons by the LLM judge and 71–75% of comparisons by human rheumatologists. Crucially, the paper explicitly notes that it omitted cross-encoder reranking for computational efficiency — a decision that mirrors the finding in VerdaSense's ablation study that a general-domain cross-encoder (trained on MS-MARCO web search data) consistently *reduced* Context Recall on clinical guideline text, suggesting that domain mismatch between the reranker training distribution and the clinical retrieval target is a generalizable limitation rather than a system-specific artefact. The EULAR RAG study's principal limitation — a guideline corpus of seventy-four documents versus VerdaSense's initial four and expanded eight — illustrates the tension between corpus breadth and controlled ablation evaluation: larger corpora improve clinical coverage but complicate attribution of metric changes to specific architectural decisions.

CARE-RAG (Potluri et al., 2025), presented at the NeurIPS 2025 Workshop on GenAI for Health, evaluates twenty LLMs on written exposure therapy (PTSD) clinical guidelines under three context conditions — correct context, noisy context, and misleading context — and three reasoning depth levels. Its central finding is that no current LLM achieves perfect reasoning fidelity even when provided with the correct retrieved context, and that binary decision points (yes/no antibiotic prescription, yes/no referral) are particularly unstable across models, with substantial variance in model outputs even for identical inputs. This finding directly motivates VerdaSense's rule-based safety checker, which enforces deterministic correctness on the most clinically consequential binary decisions — antibiotic recommendation and specialist referral — rather than relying on the generator's probabilistic reasoning for these safety-critical outputs. CARE-RAG's additional finding that reasoning-capable models (those with chain-of-thought capabilities) can introduce faithfulness penalties because their intermediate reasoning steps are not directly traceable to retrieved passages aligns with VerdaSense's observation that Qwen3:14b — a model with extended thinking capability — scores consistently lower on faithfulness than GPT-4o-mini across all nine architecture versions.

Guide-RAG (DiGiacomo et al., 2025), presented at the same NeurIPS 2025 workshop, investigates corpus curation strategies for Long COVID clinical question answering by comparing six configurations: guideline-only, guideline plus systematic reviews (GS-4), references-only, PubMed (39 million articles), web search, and no retrieval. Its central finding — that a small, well-curated corpus of one guideline and three systematic reviews outperformed all larger retrieval configurations including PubMed — is directly relevant to VerdaSense's knowledge base design choices. Larger unfiltered corpora generated authoritative-sounding but clinically misleading answers (endorsing exercise for Long COVID patients where contraindicated by retrieved sources), because the noise and domain inconsistency in large corpora undermined retrieval precision. This finding provides empirical justification for VerdaSense's strategy of maintaining a compact, manually curated guideline corpus rather than ingesting a large undifferentiated wound care literature corpus, and for prioritising targeted gap-filling (adding EWMA, ISTAP, ANZBA, and RCH guidelines to address specific identified failure patterns) over broad-scale ingestion.

The NICE guideline RAG system (Lewis et al., 2025), developed for querying the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence clinical guidelines corpus, evaluated hybrid retrieval architecture on a corpus of 10,195 text chunks derived from three hundred guidelines. When evaluated on seventy question-answer pairs, RAG-enhanced models demonstrated substantial gains in faithfulness — increased by 64.7 percentage points to 99.5% for the RAG-enhanced O4-Mini model — with clinical evaluation by seven subject matter experts further confirming that GPT-4.1 achieved 98.7% accuracy while reducing unsafe responses by 67% compared to unaugmented baseline. The study's two-stage evaluation design — automated RAGAS metrics followed by SME clinical validation — represents the methodological gold standard for clinical RAG evaluation and directly informs VerdaSense's planned evaluation pipeline combining automated RAGAS metrics with targeted clinician assessment on a subset of outputs.

### 2.6 RAG for Wound Care and Graph-Based Extensions

DressingGraphRAG (2025) represents the most domain-proximate published work to VerdaSense, proposing a graph-based RAG framework specifically designed for wound dressing research by integrating Microsoft's GraphRAG framework with LLMs to construct a knowledge graph from wound dressing literature. Unlike flat vector retrieval, graph-based retrieval preserves relational structures between wound types, dressing materials, clinical indications, and contraindications, enabling multi-hop reasoning queries that span relationships not expressible as single-chunk cosine similarity matches. The system targets wound dressing researchers rather than clinical patients, focusing on synthesising literature rather than generating patient-facing clinical recommendations, and does not integrate upstream wound classification inputs or mobile deployment. Its key limitation — the computational cost of graph construction and hierarchical community detection — distinguishes it from the lightweight, deployment-oriented architecture of VerdaSense. This limitation is also identified in Medical Graph RAG (Wu et al., 2025), which introduces MedGraphRAG — a graph-based RAG with Triple Graph Construction and U-Retrieval evaluated on nine medical question-answering benchmarks — noting that GraphRAG significantly outperforms classic RAG for complex reasoning across extensive documents, but its graph construction process is costly and requires specific domain engineering effort to apply effectively in the medical domain. For a mobile-deployed, near real-time recommendation system serving patients, the inference latency requirements of graph-based RAG architectures make flat hybrid retrieval with targeted metadata filtering a more practical design choice for the current deployment context, while graph-based approaches remain a high-value direction for future system extensions.

The broader wound care AI ecosystem has converged on several consistent findings that contextualise VerdaSense's design. First, multi-modal inputs — combining image features with structured clinical metadata — consistently outperform single-modality approaches for wound assessment tasks (Saadati Fard et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). Second, structured wound attribute extraction from images, when injected as metadata into generation prompts, provides comparable performance to raw image-VLM approaches at significantly lower computational cost, as demonstrated by the EXL Health AI Lab approach at MEDIQA-WV 2025 (Durgapraveen et al., 2025). Third, wound-specific evaluation presents challenges absent from general medical QA: dressing appropriateness cannot be assessed by answer overlap metrics alone, but requires domain-specific safety rules checking contraindication compliance, infection management guidance, and referral threshold adherence — none of which are captured by standard RAGAS metrics without supplementary rule-based validation.

### 2.7 Research Gap and Justification

The preceding review identifies a convergent set of limitations across the existing literature that collectively constitute the research gap addressed by this study. First, while clinical RAG systems have been developed for a range of medical specialties — including rheumatology (Madrid-García et al., 2025), oncology (Lammert et al., 2024), Long COVID (DiGiacomo et al., 2025), and general medicine (Zakka et al., 2024; Lewis et al., 2025) — no published system has specifically addressed wound dressing recommendation as the target clinical task, leaving a demonstrable gap in a high-burden, guideline-dense domain with significant patient safety implications. Second, existing wound care AI systems — including MEDIQA-WV 2025 participants (Yim et al., 2025), SCARWID (Xu et al., 2025), and the multimodal referral system (Saadati Fard et al., 2025) — focus on wound assessment classification outputs but do not extend to evidence-grounded *recommendation generation*, precisely the downstream clinical decision support step that this study targets. Third, no existing study has evaluated the specific impact of structured T.I.M.E. classification inputs — as opposed to free-text clinical queries or raw wound images — on RAG retrieval and generation quality, leaving the question of how T.I.M.E.-structured inputs affect Context Precision, Context Recall, and Answer Relevancy empirically unaddressed. Fourth, the integration of a dedicated rule-based clinical safety checker as a complementary evaluation layer alongside automated RAGAS metrics has not been reported for wound care RAG systems, despite CARE-RAG (Potluri et al., 2025) establishing that automated grounding metrics alone are insufficient to detect clinically unsafe binary decision outputs. Fifth, the feasibility of deploying a fully RAG-based wound dressing recommendation system within a mobile health application pipeline, receiving inputs from upstream computer vision classifiers and returning clinically usable recommendations within patient-acceptable response latency, has not been evaluated in the literature. Addressing this final gap is particularly relevant for low-resource or remote patient settings where specialist wound care access is limited and where smartphone-based health interventions have demonstrated acceptability and reach (Amani et al., 2023). VerdaSense directly targets each of these five gap dimensions, contributing both a methodological framework (the iterative dual-generator ablation study with safety-augmented RAGAS evaluation) and a deployable system (the mobile-integrated RAG recommendation pipeline) to the wound care AI and clinical RAG literature.
